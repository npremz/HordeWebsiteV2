A web design brief defines the experience you want to create before choosing the screens that will carry it. It explains who the site serves, what that person needs to accomplish, what the organisation wants to express and which constraints the project must respect.

“A homepage, team page, blog and contact form” describes containers. It does not explain the question the site must answer or how to recognise a good solution. **Custom design begins with this definition, not with an unusual shape added to a standard page.**

The six questions below can provide a starting point for your brief. A fictional cultural workshop illustrates the level of detail worth discussing. The resulting brief gives [interface art direction](/en/blog/art-direction-for-web-interfaces/) something concrete to work from.

## A useful brief can lead to building less

A brief is not a competition to list the most features. A long specification can create a feeling of control while avoiding the central question: why do we need this service?

The GOV.UK guide to [discovery](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works) encourages teams to reframe a problem rather than accept a predefined solution. Discovery can also lead to stopping work on a service that is not justified.

For a small association, a clear registration page might be a better first step than a complete members’ area. For a business, better evidence of its work might be more useful than another configurator. These are possibilities to investigate, not universal prescriptions.

The brief makes such choices possible before every idea becomes a quotation line or an approved mockup. It should help you explain why something is excluded as well as why something belongs.

## Decision one: describe a person in a situation

“Our customers” and “the general public” give design little to work with. Describe a recognisable situation: someone finds a workshop on their phone while travelling, without knowing the venue or the experience level expected.

Explain what they already know, what they are seeking, the constraints of the moment and their alternatives. The same person may want to browse on Sunday and need an immediate answer before an appointment.

Separate what you have observed from what you assume. The GOV.UK manual on [understanding user needs](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/start-by-learning-user-needs) encourages treating opinions not grounded in research as hypotheses to check.

A useful brief might say: “We suspect that experience level is a concern; check incoming questions and discuss it with participants.” That is more honest and actionable than a detailed persona with invented motivations.

Avoid turning limited evidence into a universal portrait. One support request can reveal an issue worth investigating without establishing how often it occurs.

## Decision two: name the priority task

Describe the outcome from the person’s perspective. For the workshop, it is checking the date, price and conditions before requesting a place. For a professional service, it may be understanding whether the team handles this kind of problem before making contact.

Do not confuse that task with a business metric. “Increase conversions” may be an organisational objective, but it is not what the visitor came to do. The two should connect without being treated as identical.

Add a boundary: which task is not a priority in this version? Our fictional workshop does not need to sell membership at the same moment as registration. That exclusion prevents several calls to action from competing for the same attention.

A brief can contain several journeys, but it must establish their relative importance. If everything is primary, the design team will have to decide later, often without the context required.

## Decision three: turn an impression into concrete signs

Choose a brand intention the team can discuss. “Open and curious” becomes useful when you explain how the site will show it: accessible language, varied work, unambiguous practical information and room for experimentation in the composition.

Name what you reject too. “No countdown pressuring people to book” can express how this workshop welcomes people. Other organisations might reject fabricated social proof, unverifiable claims or imagery unrelated to their actual work.

The intention should not dictate every shape. It gives designers room to explore and criteria for discussion. Two directions can answer the same brief with different visual sensibilities.

References then explain decisions instead of becoming instructions to copy. Our method for [analysing interfaces without reproducing them](/en/blog/analyse-web-interface-without-copying/) helps identify what you retain from an example and what you leave behind.

Keep the intention connected to content. A promise of openness means little if the first page assumes specialist knowledge or conceals practical conditions.

## Decision four: state constraints and responsibilities

Constraints are design material. Languages, connectivity, existing tools, budget, timing, content availability and maintenance capacity all influence what it makes sense to create.

Do not save accessibility for the end of the document. Identify situations to accommodate and checks to perform. W3C guidance on [involving users](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/involving-users/) recommends including people with disabilities in accessibility work; their participation complements rather than replaces technical evaluation.

Also describe life after handover. Who controls the domain? Who can access the code? Where are content and data held? What can be exported? Who can intervene if the provider changes? These are operational questions to settle with the team, not surprises to discover during an emergency.

For an association with limited resources, we would rather discuss honestly what its team can maintain. A smaller scope can preserve quality and independence. A public-interest project does not deserve a less considered interface because its budget is modest.

These choices influence the [real cost of a redesign](/en/blog/how-much-does-a-website-redesign-cost/). A precise scope makes a quotation easier to understand. It does not remove every unknown or guarantee a fixed price.

## Decision five: write reviewable success criteria

“The website must be intuitive” does not give anyone an acceptance test. Translate the intention into situations and expected observations without inventing a numerical target.

| Intention | Proposed review criterion |
| --- | --- |
| Understand the offer | Date, price and required experience are available before the booking action |
| Correct without starting over | An invalid field is explained and other values are retained |
| Use without a mouse | The main journey can be reached by keyboard with visible focus |
| Edit independently | The team can change a time without editing code |
| Recognise the brand | Visual decisions can be related to the intention in the brief |

Some criteria can be checked technically. Others require observing people or discussing a perception. Keep the distinction visible: displaying information does not establish that someone understands it.

If you set a performance or conversion target, record the starting point, measurement method and period. Without those details, an impressive number becomes another piece of decoration in the brief.

Also decide who accepts each criterion. Editorial accuracy, technical behaviour and brand direction may need different reviewers. One general approval should not hide missing expertise.

## Decision six: keep the unknowns visible

A good brief does not pretend to know everything. It identifies uncertainties that could change the solution: misunderstood vocabulary, assumed mobile use, an integration constraint or an unavailable content owner.

For each important unknown, name a next check and an owner. Reviewing support requests, trying a prototype with participants, testing an export and confirming a technical capability are different activities. Choose the one that reduces the relevant uncertainty.

AI can group notes, suggest wording and flag contradictions. It should not silently turn an assumption into a fact. Keep the sources of observations and review shortcuts introduced by summaries.

An empty field marked “to be defined” is therefore acceptable. It identifies work to organise, not a failed document. What matters is whether the missing answer can wait or blocks a responsible next decision.

## Keep using the brief after the first mockups

At each review, return to the six decisions. Does the proposal help the intended person? Preserve the priority task? Express the right intention? Respect the constraints? How will it be checked? What have we learned since the previous version?

The brief can change, but changes should be explicit. If a members’ area is added, record why, what is removed or postponed and who will operate it. The document becomes a memory of decisions rather than a forgotten instruction.
