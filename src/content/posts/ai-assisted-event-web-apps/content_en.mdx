Artificial intelligence does not have to be visible inside an application to transform how that application is made.

Over the past few months, it has helped me build several web apps for events, participatory workshops and presentations in record time. Their common feature was not the artificial addition of AI to every screen. Their value came from answering a precise need for a particular audience at a particular moment.

Not long ago, I probably would not have considered building the same level of software for such a short period of use. The design, development and synchronisation effort would have been difficult to justify.

When the need is tightly scoped, it is now possible to produce a specialised, coherent and sufficiently reliable application in about two weeks. The real change is less about a new feature than a new way of producing the product.

## AI as a production tool, not a mandatory feature

Discussions about AI and web applications usually turn to chatbots, recommendation engines or automatically generated content.

That is not the change I find most interesting.

The deeper shift is in how the software is produced. AI shortens the path from an idea to a prototype, accelerates interface creation and data connections, and makes it easier to iterate before the experience feels coherent.

These tools are no longer limited to completing a few lines of code. [GitHub Copilot’s documentation](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/copilot-on-github/use-copilot-agents/research-plan-iterate) describes agents that can analyse a project, prepare a plan and iterate on complete changes.

This does not mean AI instinctively understands an event or the people attending it. Nor can it decide which idea deserves to be built. Its main contribution is reducing the cost of exploration and execution.

The time saved can be spent on scoping, testing and the details that make the tool genuinely useful. That distinction matters because [an interface produced quickly does not guarantee a good experience](/en/blog/ai-can-create-interfaces-not-experiences/).

## A digital control room for workshops at 42

I recently ran two introductory coding workshops for 42. To support them, I developed web apps designed specifically around the way those workshops unfolded.

They did more than display information. Together, they formed a digital control room for the event.

From a central interface, I could:

- manage the composition and progress of the teams;
- control the interfaces used as workshop support;
- organise the different stages of the experience;
- provide game mechanics suited to the learning objectives;
- synchronise the data with the room’s display system.

Participants received a coherent experience while I retained a complete view of the workshop and its progress.

The application connected three dimensions that could easily have been scattered across separate tools: management, teaching and the live staging of the event. An action inside the interface could immediately change the information shown to teams or on the room’s displays.

Its value came precisely from being bespoke. It was not trying to become a generic platform for every workshop. It was designed around the way these two sessions needed to work.

## Turning a startup presentation into an interactive experience

I used the same approach to support a startup during a presentation to investors.

Instead of relying solely on a linear slide deck, we built a web app that presented the project, its operation and its important information through an accessible, structured interface.

The platform was also connected to a model prepared from the startup’s information. Investors could ask questions and receive answers grounded in the project content.

In this case, AI was visible in the final experience. The more significant change, however, was our ability to produce the whole system quickly: the presentation, navigation, data structure and question-and-answer interface.

The web app did more than describe the product. It became part of the demonstration and allowed each investor to explore the information through their own questions.

## Why this kind of application is becoming practical

An event web app can create a great deal of value during a relatively short life. It may be used intensively for only a day or a few weeks while still playing an important role in the success of that moment.

Until recently, the difficulty was justifying the cost of such a specialised tool. A large part of the work had to go into technical structure, supporting interfaces and connections before the central idea could even be tested.

AI changes that balance. Within a controlled production process, it can help a team:

- explore several technical approaches;
- turn a journey into a working interface more quickly;
- produce repetitive software structures;
- connect data sources;
- accelerate corrections;
- test several variations without restarting the project.

A web app also has a practical advantage at an event: it can open from a link or QR code without forcing visitors through a native app download. When delivered as a Progressive Web App, it may also be installed and provide selected offline capabilities, depending on browser support. [MDN explains both these capabilities and their limitations](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps/Guides/What_is_a_progressive_web_app).

This combination of immediate access and faster production makes short-lived software practical in situations where the previous effort would have been difficult to justify.

## Two weeks does not mean two weeks for every product

The timeframe depends entirely on scope.

An application involving complex payments, highly sensitive data, many legacy systems or a large number of user scenarios will need more work. AI does not remove security, testing or responsibility from software development.

A timeframe of about two weeks becomes credible when several conditions are met:

- the event and its audience are known;
- the primary problem is clearly identified;
- there are only a few essential journeys;
- the necessary roles and data are defined;
- integrations remain controlled;
- decisions can be made quickly.

Quality then depends less on the number of features than on the precision of the scope.

AI makes adding another screen or option remarkably easy. A team still has to resist that convenience. A product that is fast to build can become confusing just as quickly when nobody protects its main purpose.

## Which events can benefit from a bespoke web app?

This approach can support far more than a trade show or large conference.

An event web app can be designed for:

- a participatory workshop with teams and stages;
- a hackathon with challenges, scores and live displays;
- a training session with interactive exercises;
- an investor presentation;
- a product launch;
- a guided or scripted visitor journey;
- an assembly with voting and contributions;
- an internal event with a personalised programme;
- an exhibition connecting physical and digital content.

In each case, the right question is not “which features can we add?” It is “which moment do we want to make simpler, clearer or more engaging?”

The right scope can then be compared with relevant work in [our digital project portfolio](/en/projets/) without trying to reproduce a generic platform.

## An event web app is also an MVP

These projects follow [the logic of a minimum viable product](/en/services/mvp-development-brussels/).

We do not begin by building a complete platform. We identify one central use, develop the smallest system that can support it properly and put it into a real situation.

The method can be summarised in seven steps:

1. Understand how the event unfolds.
2. Identify the moments where a digital tool can genuinely help.
3. Define the interfaces for participants and organisers.
4. Build the first working journey.
5. Connect the data and display systems.
6. Test the complete system in realistic conditions.
7. Keep only what contributes to the experience.

The event then becomes a strong validation environment. The application can remain temporary, return for the next edition or evolve into a broader product.

## Shorter-lived software can still be ambitious

AI allows us to reconsider what deserves to become software.

An application no longer needs to target thousands of users or remain in operation for years to justify its development. It can be designed for one date, one location, one group and one specific experience.

That may be the most interesting change. It is not the multiplication of intelligent features, but the possibility of producing digital tools that sit much closer to real situations.

A workshop can have its own game mechanics. A presentation can become interactive. An organiser can control several interfaces from one place. A screen in the room can immediately reflect what is happening inside the application.

The software may be temporary. Its value can be immediate.
