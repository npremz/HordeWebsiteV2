Artificial intelligence can already produce a credible interface in minutes. Give it a prompt and it can generate a homepage, dashboard, registration form or application prototype.

The result often looks polished. It can even be impressive. Yet an interface that works in a screenshot is not necessarily pleasant to use. It may contain too much information, demand too many decisions or complicate an action that should feel obvious.

This is the limit of validation based on generation alone: composing screens does not establish that someone understands the journey. A model can help identify an ambiguous button or an unnecessary interruption. Its suggestions still need to be checked against real context and use.

AI can accelerate the design of a digital product. It should not decide the experience on its own. This is different from simply [making a website stand out](/en/blog/how-to-make-your-website-stand-out-2026/): a distinctive identity earns attention, while a good experience helps someone reach a goal without unnecessary obstacles.

## An interface is not an experience

An interface includes the information, controls and behaviours through which someone interacts with a system. It is not limited to what we see: focus, screen reader announcements and feedback after an action are part of it too.

The experience is what happens when someone tries to accomplish something through that interface.

The difference may sound subtle, but it changes how a website or piece of software should be designed. A form can look impeccable and become frustrating because it asks for information the user does not have nearby. A dashboard can display every available metric while making the only important one difficult to find. A homepage can look complete while delaying access to its main action.

The problem is not necessarily the colour palette or the quality of the code. It is the journey.

The [GOV.UK design principles](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles) express this distinction clearly: start with user needs, understand context and build a service rather than merely a website. They also note that making a service genuinely simple is much harder than making it look simple.

An experience can therefore never be reduced to one screen. It unfolds over time, with a goal, a context and sometimes real consequences for the person using the product. Speed is part of that lived experience too. A clear journey that responds slowly is still painful, as our article on [what makes a website genuinely fast](/en/blog/what-is-a-really-fast-website/) explains.

## Why does AI produce so much distracting content?

A vague request for a “complete interface” can encourage an accumulation of familiar conventions: navigation, a large headline, arguments, cards, testimonials, FAQs, buttons and a newsletter invitation. Results depend on the model, context and constraints supplied; this is not a measured rule for every tool.

Each element may appear relevant when considered alone. Their accumulation creates a problem: the user must scan, interpret and dismiss more information before understanding what they can do.

Distracting content is not always unnecessary copy. It can also be:

- a second button competing with the primary action;
- a feature added because competitors have one;
- an explanation compensating for confusing navigation;
- an animation drawing attention at the wrong moment;
- a dashboard showing data without supporting a decision;
- an extra step that reassures the organisation rather than the user.

The problem is not that AI cannot produce a minimalist interface. It can. Without reliable information about the situation, however, it lacks a sufficient basis for deciding which elements are essential.

It can estimate what looks conventional from the context it receives. It cannot determine what truly deserves to exist without a solid understanding of the need and observed behaviour.

This weakness is not unique to AI. Human teams also add features to satisfy multiple departments, anticipate every scenario or avoid a difficult decision. AI simply makes that accumulation faster and cheaper to produce. A website that grows through years of additions eventually creates [hidden maintenance and conversion costs](/en/blog/what-your-website-really-costs/) as well.

## The trap of the plausible interface

An AI-generated interface can feel convincing because it uses the right visual codes. Everything appears to sit where it belongs. A card has an icon, a button uses an action verb, the dashboard contains charts and the form confirms that its fields have been completed.

That visual coherence can hide much more important questions:

- What happens when the user makes a mistake?
- Do they understand what will be submitted before confirming?
- Can they go back without losing their work?
- Does the important information remain visible on mobile?
- Does the journey work with a screen reader or only with a mouse?
- What happens when the data are incomplete?
- Is the same action still understandable after several weeks away from the product?

Microsoft’s [Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/ai-guidelines/) deliberately cover several moments: before interaction, during use, when the system is wrong and over time. This structure is a useful reminder that an experience cannot be evaluated only in its ideal state.

A mock-up usually presents the best-case scenario. A real experience also includes interruptions, errors, reversals, delays and changing habits. Product quality often becomes most visible in these less photogenic situations.

## Good experiences often depend on what gets removed

AI makes addition extremely easy. A new section, page variation or extra feature can be produced almost immediately.

Experience design requires a different question: what can be removed without preventing the user from reaching their goal?

Removing something does not mean arbitrarily impoverishing the product. It means establishing a hierarchy. Secondary information can appear only when it becomes useful. Advanced options can remain discreet for most people. A page can focus on one decision rather than presenting every possibility at once.

Reduction can improve accessibility too. The [W3C guidance on cognitive accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/cognitive/) covers understandable content, predictable behaviour and interfaces that help people navigate, avoid mistakes and correct them. These principles are essential for some users and also make products easier to use when people are tired, distracted or under pressure.

Designing less requires more understanding. To remove an element, you need to know what it does, who relies on it and what will happen in its absence.

Generating ten variations is easy. Choosing the one that creates the least friction requires judgment.

## What generation does not validate about use

AI can analyse data and identify patterns. It can summarise interviews, suggest hypotheses or compare several journeys. These are useful capabilities, but a simulated reaction does not replace observation of relevant people.

Concern about an important payment, fatigue from repetitive work and urgency before an appointment are contexts to document. Describing them plausibly does not establish that the product responds to them well.

A pause, a hesitant cursor or the words “I suppose I should click here” may deserve attention during an observation. Interpreting them requires checking what is happening rather than automatically assigning an emotion or intention to the participant.

These details are difficult to turn into universal rules because their meaning depends on context. A very short journey may be ideal for an everyday purchase but not reassuring enough for a consequential decision. A large amount of information may obstruct a first-time visitor and be essential to an experienced professional. Automation may save time for one person while making another feel that they have lost control.

Google’s [People + AI Guidebook](https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook-v2/) recommends beginning with real user needs, observing behaviour and validating assumptions through user research. It also addresses mental models, control, trust and graceful failure, none of which is automatically solved by generating an interface.

AI can support this work. It cannot close the feedback loop on behalf of users.

## Human instinct is not enough either

Saying that experience remains human does not mean that a designer should rely only on instinct. The designer’s experience is not the user’s experience.

Someone who has worked on an interface for several weeks already knows its vocabulary, logic and shortcuts. They may no longer see the ambiguities that stop a first-time visitor.

The alternative to fully AI-generated design is not design based on personal taste. It is a process grounded in observation. That process can combine:

- interviews to understand goals and context;
- prototypes to test an idea before building it;
- usability tests to observe hesitation;
- analytics to identify abandonment points;
- requests sent to customer support;
- feedback from teams that assist users every day;
- accessibility testing with real people.

The [GOV.UK user research manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/start-by-learning-user-needs) recommends treating opinions about users that do not come from research as assumptions that still need to be proven. The rule applies equally to human intuition and model output.

## How to use AI without delegating the experience

AI becomes particularly useful when it enters a process whose objective is already clear.

It can accelerate the exploration of variations, create a first structure, help document a journey, suggest edge cases or transform a prototype quickly after a test. It can also challenge a solution by identifying redundant information or areas where multiple actions compete.

Responsibility for the experience must remain explicit. Before accepting a generated interface, a team should be able to answer five questions:

1. What precise problem must this interface solve?
2. What is the user’s primary action?
3. Which elements could delay or complicate that action?
4. What observations support our choices?
5. How will we verify that the journey works in practice?

If these answers are missing, producing more screens solves nothing. It merely gives a more finished form to assumptions that remain fragile.

AI should shorten the distance between an idea and its test, not remove the test. This discipline can also prevent an expensive [website redesign becoming unavoidable](/en/blog/how-much-does-a-website-redesign-cost/) because the interface was allowed to grow through accumulation.

This logic becomes particularly concrete in the events sector. An [event web app produced with AI assistance](/en/blog/ai-assisted-event-web-apps/) can be built quickly around a workshop or presentation when the need and critical journeys remain tightly scoped.

## A six-part human review

The September update adds the workshop at the start of this article and this review framework. In the fictional example, you can show a clear hierarchy or three competing invitations. The copy, price and practical details stay the same. Additional invitations are comparison elements, not functioning registration journeys.

We suggest reviewing six dimensions before approving an interface, regardless of how it was produced.

| Dimension | Question | Record to keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task | Which decision is someone here to make? | A sentence describing the intended outcome |
| Hierarchy | Which elements support or compete with that decision? | An annotated view identifying priorities |
| Understanding | Are the words and information sufficient? | Observed ambiguities and remaining assumptions |
| Behaviour | What happens after an action or an error? | A journey performed in the browser |
| Access | Can the task be completed by keyboard and on a narrow screen? | Conditions checked and obstacles found |
| Responsibility | Who owns the choices and arranges the next check? | A named owner and an explicit decision |

Three observations are directly visible in the workshop. First, the time and price appear before the action. Second, the additional invitations do not explain this particular workshop. Third, the button offers details rather than announcing a booking. Those choices can be inspected in the code and the rendered page.

Interpretation remains separate. We suspect that limiting competing actions helps someone focus on choosing the workshop. We have not measured an effect on bookings or established a winning variant. In a real project, a secondary invitation might prove useful for some visitors.

[Art direction](/en/blog/art-direction-for-web-interfaces/) can then build character without obscuring those priorities. [Design and development working together](/en/blog/design-and-web-development-together/) makes states beyond the screenshot inspectable. [Analysing documented references](/en/blog/analyse-web-interface-without-copying/) can inform choices without treating an established pattern as proof for your audience.

This framework is neither a complete accessibility audit nor a user study. It organises an initial review and identifies what remains to be checked. That is already more useful than approving an interface because it looks finished.

## Design less, but design better

AI can already create very good interfaces when it receives useful context and quality constraints. But an experience does not emerge from the ability to produce alone.

It emerges from the ability to observe, question and choose. It requires an understanding of what someone is actually trying to accomplish, followed by the removal of everything that gets in the way.

The role of the designer and product team does not disappear. It moves. Their value no longer lies only in producing screens. It lies in defining the right problem, organising information, evaluating consequences and confronting the product with reality.

An interface can be generated. An experience still has to be lived.
