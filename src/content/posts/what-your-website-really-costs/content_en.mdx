The real cost of a website is not limited to its build invoice and hosting bill. An underperforming website mainly costs the business through what it prevents: enquiries that never arrive, less efficient campaigns, wasted staff time and technical debt that makes every future change more expensive.

Those losses cannot be inferred from a generic score. They have to be modelled with your data and verified across the journey. Speed, for example, should be compared with abandonment and conversion instead of judged alone. Our guide to [what makes a website genuinely fast](/en/blog/what-is-a-really-fast-website/) provides the right technical benchmarks.

## A simple formula for estimating the opportunity cost

For a website that generates commercial enquiries, an initial scenario can be calculated as follows:

**monthly qualified visits × conversion-rate gap × average gross profit per new client × 12**

Consider a deliberately simple example:

* 1,200 qualified visits per month;
* a current conversion rate of 1.5%;
* an improved scenario at 2%;
* €800 in average gross profit per signed client.

The 0.5-point gap represents six additional enquiries or sales per month if every conversion becomes a client. Under that maximum scenario, the annual opportunity is €57,600.

That amount is not a promise of recoverable revenue. In practice, not every form submission becomes a client, attribution is imperfect and traffic quality varies. The formula should therefore include the qualification and close rates:

**visits × lead conversion × qualification × close rate × gross profit**

The point is not to produce an impressive number. It is to expose the assumptions, then identify which one the available evidence can genuinely improve.

## Cost #1: conversions that disappear without a trace

A form broken on iOS, a hidden button, an unclear proposition or a slow page will not trigger a commercial alert. The visitor simply leaves.

The company examples collected in [web.dev’s performance case studies](https://web.dev/case-studies) show that some businesses recorded conversion improvements after improving their experience. They demonstrate that an effect is possible, not that speed single-handedly explains every sale.

To isolate friction, segment results by landing page, device, source and journey stage. A drop concentrated on mobile calls for a different investigation from a weak conversion rate across every channel.

## Cost #2: paid acquisition that leaks

A campaign can have a strong click-through rate and still perform poorly if the landing page does not continue the promise made in the advert. Each euro then buys a visit that meets an unclear message, a slow load or an unnecessarily long form.

The cost does not sit only in the media budget. It appears in the gap between the price of traffic and the number of useful commercial conversations created.

Before increasing campaign spend, measure:

* the cost per qualified visit;
* the rate at which people actually reach the page;
* progression toward the primary action;
* completed forms;
* the quality and value of leads recorded in the CRM.

Sometimes a [landing page designed around the campaign](/en/services/creation-landing-page/) fixes the underlying problem more effectively than a higher media budget.

## Cost #3: organic visibility that stops progressing

A website can be indexed and still limit its own visibility through overlapping content, thin service pages, missing internal links, a poor mobile experience or recurring technical issues.

It would be excessive to blame an SEO plateau on one Core Web Vital. [Google says page experience contributes to its systems](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience), while also making clear that content relevance remains central. Technical improvements remove an obstacle; they cannot replace a good response to search intent.

The SEO cost can be investigated in Search Console through lost impressions, declining positions, important pages that are not indexed and queries that receive visibility but no clicks. Those signals then have to be connected to editorial and technical causes.

## Cost #4: maintenance time and technical risk

An old website may remain online while requiring intervention for every small change. Add the time spent on updates, backups, recurring bugs, duplicate entry and manual quality checks. Multiply those hours by their fully loaded cost and hidden maintenance becomes measurable.

An accumulation of plugins and dependencies also adds risk. The [OWASP Top 10:2025](https://owasp.org/Top10/2025/A03_2025-Software_Supply_Chain_Failures/) lists software supply chain failures among its major risks and explicitly includes outdated, unmaintained and unpatchable components.

That does not make a CMS or plugin inherently bad. The problem begins when nobody knows which dependencies are active, who maintains them and how an update will be tested.

## Cost #5: debt paid during the next major change

An incoherent information architecture, content that cannot be exported, a domain controlled by a supplier or a CMS that cannot be migrated may not disrupt today’s work. They become expensive when the company changes its offer, adds a language or suddenly needs to replace the website.

The same principle applies commercially. As with an audience built entirely on a social platform, a company loses room to manoeuvre when it does not control access and data. Our comparison of [websites and social media](/en/blog/why-have-a-website-when-social-media-works/) explains that distinction between rented distribution and a controlled asset.

## The evidence to collect before making a decision

A useful audit connects technical measurements with business outcomes.

| Evidence | Question it helps answer |
| --- | --- |
| Analytics or audience measurement | Which pages and devices lose the most visitors? |
| Search Console | Which pages are gaining or losing organic visibility? |
| CRM or sales tracking | Which forms become real opportunities and clients? |
| Real-user Core Web Vitals | Which visitors experience slowness or instability? |
| Support tickets and staff time | What does routine maintenance consume? |
| Technical invoices | Which services, licences and interventions accumulate? |

Use a representative period, record the limits of each source and avoid confusing correlation with causation. If evidence is missing, the first improvement may be to install simple measurement before changing the website.

## Audit, optimisation or redesign?

A focused correction is often enough when the website still has a sound structure and a few bottlenecks concentrate most of the loss: heavy media, a failing form, third-party scripts or imprecise service pages. [Website optimisation](/en/services/optimisation-site-web/) may then create more value than a rebuild.

A redesign becomes relevant when several issues are connected: confusing architecture, an obstructive CMS, design that interferes with the journey, unmanageable content and an unavoidable migration. Before committing, compare the investment with the [realistic cost of a redesign in Belgium](/en/blog/how-much-does-a-website-redesign-cost/).

The right diagnosis does not begin with “should we rebuild everything?” It starts with three questions:

1. Where is value being lost today?
2. What evidence connects that loss to the website?
3. What is the least expensive intervention that can test an improvement?

## Turn an invisible cost into a plan of action

A website that remains online is not necessarily a healthy website. To decide without either exaggerating or minimising the issue, model the journey, document the assumptions and rank fixes by impact, effort and risk.
