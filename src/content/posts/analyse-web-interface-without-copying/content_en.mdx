To analyse a web interface without copying it, start with the task it organises, inspect its decisions and name the principle you might reuse. A colour, rounded corner or transition may appeal to you. That does not yet explain why someone understands what to do.

A reference becomes useful when it answers a specific question: how do we show unfinished work? How can someone recover from an error? What should a service say before any content exists? **Borrow the reasoning, not the identity.**

This complements [web art direction](/en/blog/art-direction-for-web-interfaces/). Familiar patterns can support usability without requiring every organisation to look the same. We will examine three documented public references. The examples below illustrate the documented principles; they are not findings from user tests.

## Choose references for the questions they answer

A gallery of attractive screens can help explore tone, material and composition. It is less useful for understanding a journey when it shows neither the preceding step, the next step nor an error state.

Choose a reference whose behaviour you can inspect or read about. Note the likely person, task, entry point and next step. When something is unknown, write “unknown”. A visible gap is more useful than a convincing story invented to fill it.

A complex dashboard is not automatically a good reference for booking a workshop. An express checkout may be inappropriate for an application completed over several days. Similarity of task can matter more than similarity of sector.

You might learn from a public service’s error recovery and a developer tool’s first-use experience. Neither choice requires you to adopt that organisation’s tone or reproduce the whole product.

Save enough context with the reference to remember why you selected it. A cropped image without a task or source tends to become a vague instruction to “make something like this”.

## Separate observation, interpretation and hypothesis

We use three kinds of notes. An observation describes something you can point to. An interpretation offers an explanation. A hypothesis identifies what you would want to test in your own situation.

| Level | Example note | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | A summary lists fields needing correction before the form | That every visitor notices it |
| Interpretation | It may help someone understand the extent of the problem | That it reduces abandonment |
| Hypothesis | On our long form, a summary could help people resume correction | That the same pattern helps with a single field |

This distinction prevents a common shortcut: “They use it, therefore it works.” Even a respected organisation may have different constraints, technical history or invisible compromises.

Do not invent numbers to make an analysis appear more rigorous. Without relevant published measurements, describe the mechanism and its limits. The purpose is a better-supported decision, not a conversion promise.

It is also fine to have a visual preference. Just label it as a preference. The problem starts when taste is presented as evidence about other people’s behaviour.

## Reference one: make unfinished work visible

The [GOV.UK task list](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/task-list/) associates tasks with statuses. Its documentation reserves the pattern for sufficiently complex journeys that may span sessions or allow a flexible order. It encourages simplification first.

Consider a fictional application: contact details are complete, documents are missing and submission is not yet available. The interesting decision is not the status colour. It is making remaining work visible alongside each task.

A reusable rule might be: when someone resumes an interrupted process, show what is complete, what is missing and what blocks the next step. That rule can survive a different visual identity, vocabulary and layout.

By contrast, a booking that requires only a date and a name may not need a task list. Adding one could make the process look more complicated without helping anyone. Good analysis can conclude, “This pattern is not for us.”

The question to investigate would be whether someone can return after an interruption and continue without rereading the whole application. Describing the proposed structure does not answer that question.

## Reference two: support repair, not just failure reporting

The [GOV.UK error summary](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-summary/) collects problems, links them to relevant fields and specifies moving focus to the summary. Summary messages and field messages should match.

Consider a missing date. “Check before continuing” frames a repairable situation. “Date: enter your preferred day” identifies the expected information. It does more than report a generic failure.

The principle we retain is a relationship: provide an overview of the problem and a precise route to where it can be corrected. Copying only a red box, without its links, focus behaviour and associated messages, would reproduce the appearance while losing the interaction.

This matters when [AI generates a plausible interface](/en/blog/ai-can-create-interfaces-not-experiences/) too. An error state may look convincing in a picture while being difficult to reach with a keyboard or impossible to fix without losing input.

For a real form, inspect field navigation, announcements, retained values and the state after correction. The explanation here is not a ready-to-deploy implementation of the complete GOV.UK component.

## Reference three: explain why content is absent

[Primer’s Blankslate component](https://primer.style/product/components/blankslate/) structures an empty state around an explanation and, where appropriate, a primary or secondary action. It gives meaning to an area that would otherwise be blank.

In our fictional workshop space, the message explains that no workshops have been saved and suggests creating the first one. It connects a situation with a next step instead of filling the space with decoration that leaves someone wondering whether loading has finished.

But “empty” describes several different conditions. A first visit, a search with no matches, insufficient permissions and a service failure need different explanations. Giving them all the same reassuring message may hide the actual problem.

Our rule would be: explain why content is missing and offer an action compatible with that cause. If the cause is unknown, do not invent it. A “create” action would be inappropriate when items exist but cannot be loaded.

The review question is whether someone understands the state and sees a relevant next step, not simply whether the illustration is pleasant.

## Turn the analysis into a project decision

Finish with a short note containing the problem, reference, observation, retained principle, differences in context and next check. Explicitly name what you are not taking from the reference.

For example: “We will explore a status beside each stage to support returning visitors. We are not adopting GOV.UK’s appearance. Our process is shorter, so we will first check whether a single page would be enough.” A designer and developer can both act on that statement.

If the team cannot describe the differences in context, return to the [experience brief](/en/blog/web-design-brief-before-screens/). A reference cannot supply your knowledge of the audience or decide the organisation’s priority for you.

You can then make a small prototype that tests only the uncertain relationship. There is little value in reproducing a complete product when the question is whether a status helps someone resume a task. A focused prototype is easier to question and change.

Successful analysis may lead to fewer components. It may also show that a conventional pattern should stay while character comes from elsewhere: content, composition, evidence and details specific to the organisation. That is how a [website can stand out without becoming confusing](/en/blog/how-to-make-your-website-stand-out-2026/).

Keep the source and the decision together when you hand the work over. Otherwise, a future reviewer may see only the visual reference and mistake the retained principle for an instruction to copy its styling.

If you have collected references but cannot make a choice, [let’s discuss the journey you want to create](/en/contact/). The useful starting question is not “Which site do we like best?” It is “Which decision does this reference help us make?”
