You are preparing a new website when you come across a reference that makes you raise your ambitions. The work commands attention, every detail feels considered, and moving from one project to the next is part of the pleasure. You want that same level of quality for your business. But how do you describe it without asking for a copy?

To use a website as inspiration without copying it, start by naming what creates that impression. Then ask how you can achieve an equally considered result for your own identity, content and visitors. Let’s see what that means through one example: Vucko’s website.

## Vucko showcases its craft from the homepage

[Vucko](https://vucko.co/) is a studio that creates motion identities for brands. The footer credits Locomotive for the website. We are looking at it here on desktop: a large area devoted to the studio’s work, with a navigation column on the right.

![Vucko’s homepage on desktop: an introduction to the studio, a large animated preview of the Google Next project and a navigation column on the right.](/src/assets/images/blog/analyse-web-interface-without-copying/vucko-home.png "Vucko’s homepage, captured on desktop on September 15, 2026. The large visual is a still from a playing video; the screenshot cannot convey its motion. Click to enlarge.")

The introduction takes only a few lines. The Google Next project then fills most of the available space, with a video showing the work in context. A small card on this colorful surface contains the project name and a link to it. The gray background and black text remain understated around it.

In our view, this composition gives the studio’s work a presence that small thumbnails would struggle to match. The quality of the work becomes part of the website experience. Motion has a clear subject here: it is exactly what Vucko creates.

For your own website, the idea worth retaining is to give your craft enough space to be seen. Imagine an interior architecture practice. A wide view could establish the atmosphere of a place, then a close-up could reveal a joint or material. Video is not essential if photography tells the project’s story more effectively.

This intention leads to questions of [web art direction](/en/blog/art-direction-for-web-interfaces/): which images should you produce, how should you frame them and how much space should they occupy? Copying the gray background and rounded corners would answer none of these questions.

## Moving between projects is part of the experience

From the right-hand column, we opened the [Spotify Wrapped 2025 project](https://vucko.co/work/spotify-wrapped-2025). The main content changes, while the clock, site links and project list remain within the same side panel.

![The Spotify Wrapped 2025 project open on Vucko: the main content has changed, but the navigation column remains on the right.](/src/assets/images/blog/analyse-web-interface-without-copying/vucko-project.png "After opening Spotify Wrapped 2025 from the side list on September 15, 2026. Compare the right-hand column with the homepage: the setting remains recognizable despite the change of project. Click or tap the image to enlarge.")

We find this continuity as interesting as the visuals. The website lets you browse a collection of work within a setting that stays familiar. It offers a direction for a portfolio where someone can spend time with one project, then choose another.

The details also shape the character of this space. The clock shows Toronto time, and each project has its own small rounded card. These choices give the navigation area a distinct personality. You can appreciate that without attributing a commercial gain to it. The pleasure of exploring an interface matters too.

To adapt the intention, your agency could explore keeping a selection of work within reach while someone views a project. The design of that navigation would still need to be invented. A clock or a column identical to Vucko’s would not necessarily make sense for you.

## Images draw attention; project details explain the work

On the same Spotify page, below the video, two text blocks introduce the context and the work delivered. Vucko describes its contribution to the motion identity for Wrapped 2025, created in collaboration with Spotify’s in-house creative team.

![Vucko’s Spotify Wrapped 2025 page after scrolling: the Context and Outcome columns explain the background and the studio’s contribution.](/src/assets/images/blog/analyse-web-interface-without-copying/vucko-context.png "The Spotify Wrapped 2025 page, captured after scrolling on September 15, 2026. The Context and Outcome text complements the visuals by clarifying Vucko’s role in the project. Click or tap the image to enlarge.")

The visuals invite you to look; the text helps explain what the studio actually designed. For someone looking for a partner, that precision is part of what makes the portfolio useful.

Return to our fictional interior architecture practice. After showing photographs of a restaurant, it could explain that it reworked the circulation and designed the furniture, while crediting the other contributors. That says more about how the practice works than a sequence of images accompanied only by the venue’s name.

It does not need clients as widely known as Vucko’s to describe its projects precisely. It does need strong content and honest attribution of the work. A layout cannot invent either.

## What you can ask your agency for

With these observations, your request can become more specific than “we want a premium website.” For example:

“We like how much space Vucko gives its work and how we can discover several projects within a consistent setting. On our website, we want to show the spaces we design, right down to their details, then explain our contribution. We want that level of care, expressed through an identity of our own.”

This gives the design team room to explore while setting a clear level of ambition. Add it to your [website brief](/en/blog/web-design-brief-before-screens/), along with screenshots that show the relevant moments.

The next step is to check what this ambition requires in your case. Are your photographs detailed enough to be shown at a large scale? How many projects do you want to feature? On mobile, how can visitors reach the selection without reducing the images to thumbnails?

A first prototype, using one real project and its content, would let you discuss these choices. It would also be the time to review transitions in motion, keyboard access and loading on mobile. Our visual reading of Vucko is not an audit of its accessibility or performance, nor is it evidence of business results.

**A reference sets a standard; your project gives it a form of its own.** If you have found a website that expresses the quality you are looking for, [let’s talk about what you want people to feel](/en/contact/) and the content that could create that experience.
